# Series bible and style guide for Book Three

You are drafting ONE chapter of the third book in a series. Read this whole file, then read the exemplar chapters listed at the end, then write.

## The series

Book One: *Asking the Right Question* (never quoted directly; referred to as "the first book"). It was about asking better questions of people: colleagues, children, students. Its five tools, each a single question:
- The Pause: *What might I be missing?*
- The Reframe: *What am I assuming this problem is about?*
- The Perspective Shift: *How might this look from the other side?*
- The Condition Question: *What conditions might be shaping this behaviour?*
- The Small Experiment: *What is one small way to test this?*
Its recurring ideas and stories: loud questions versus quiet questions (loud ones decide in advance what will be noticed: *Why is this late? Why won't you just do it?*); "the most dangerous moment is certainty arrived at too soon"; questions that sort versus questions that open (a sorting question checks whether you've arrived, an opening question keeps you moving); the narrowing question students learn to ask, *What does the teacher want?*; the homework story (a ten-year-old avoids homework, turns out to be stuck at the first step and afraid to say so; the useful question was *Where did it start to feel confusing?*); the lunch tray (fruit went untouched; the answer was to move the fruit, not lecture the child, i.e. change the condition not the child); the teacher who put down the red pen and said *Say more. What made you think that?* and then waited; "better questions aren't about influence, they're about attention"; asking well is a threshold, not a destination; it ended with the question *What kind of thinking am I bringing into this?*

Book Two: *Asking the Right Prompt* (referred to as "the second book"). It carried the same habits into AI chat windows. Its ideas: the reversal (answers became cheap, so the question is the only scarce input); the eleven-second lesson plan that was correct, complete and useless because it was for a class that didn't exist; the anatomy of a prompt (what am I actually trying to do, what does it need to know that I take for granted, what would make the answer unusable, what shape should the answer take); the reply matters more than the first prompt (*closer, but not quite*); the Reverse Prompt (*Do not write this for me. Ask me one question at a time until I can explain the argument myself. Then tell me where my reasoning is weakest.*); prompts that open rather than sort; checking answers the way five disciplines do (philosophy: define your terms; science: ask for the case against; journalism: ask who is saying this; law: ask in sequence; art: hold the question open); the tool fills every gap you leave with an average; a summary is a claim about a document; describe what the tool cannot see; four things that stay human: choosing the question, weighing the answer, deciding, owning the result; disclosure (*Did you write this?* and the small flinch); "the tool can take on more of the work than ever; it cannot take on any of the responsibility".

Book Three (this one): **Teaching the Right Question**, subtitle *How Students Learn to Ask, Think and Check When Answers Are Instant*. It brings the habits into the classroom and the home. Its readers are teachers, parents, and older students. Its central claim: when every student carries an answer key, the thing worth teaching is no longer the answer but the question, the checking, and the judgement, and those can be taught the same way anything is taught, in small repeated moments. Part One is about the classroom and the home in general and works in any subject. Part Two is a set of short field guides, one per subject (science, mathematics, English) plus one on building small classroom tools and one on assessment and honesty. Refer to earlier books as "the first book" and "the second book". Never invent a title for a fourth book.

## Voice and style (match these exactly)

- First person, warm, unhurried, plain. A reflective, experienced educator talking to a colleague. Occasional first-person asides: "I've done this myself.", "It took me longer than I'd like to admit.", "For me it's usually the last one."
- Occasional direct question to the reader: "Have you noticed this in your own classroom yet?"
- British spelling: colour, behaviour, organise, practise (verb), judgement (mostly), fulfils, programme, maths. School vocabulary: Primary 4, Primary 5, Secondary 2, Secondary 3, PE, the staff room, form teacher, tuition, the canteen, the parent-teacher meeting, the mid-year exam, a worksheet, marking. Singapore flavour without ever naming the country or its ministry.
- Stories before theories. Each chapter opens with a specific person in a specific moment ("A teacher I know...", "A Secondary 2 student...", "A parent at the kitchen table..."). Short paragraphs. Concrete details (the time of day, what was on the screen, what was typed). Never a lecture.
- Vignettes inside sections start with a bold lead: **A science teacher marking practicals.** then the story.
- What people type is shown in italics inline: *Write me a lesson plan on fractions.* Prompts offered to the reader are set apart as a blockquote (a `>` line), one or two sentences, no quotation marks, no labels.
- Takeaway lines appear after the opening story and after some sections, on their own line: `🔍 Takeaway: ...` One sentence, sometimes two. Sparing: two to four per chapter.
- Sentences: mostly short. Vary rhythm. NO em-dashes anywhere. No semicolons in ordinary prose (they appear only inside single-sentence prompts in the appendix). No bullet lists. No numbered lists. No tables. No headers deeper than `##`. No bold except the vignette leads and the named items in a short "four things" style passage.
- Pattern the reader should feel: the first attempt reaches for control, speed, or a finished product and gets exactly what it asked for; the second attempt slows down, says what is true, and points at understanding rather than fixing. "The tool didn't improve. The question did." Say this in fresh words each time, never the same sentence twice.
- The AI tool is called "the tool", "a chat window", "the window". Vendor names only in passing and only if needed (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, NotebookLM, Copilot). Never "LLM", never "generative AI", never "prompt engineering". "Fluent" rather than "intelligent".
- Never moralise, never panic about cheating. The book's stance on students using AI: the essay was never the point, the thinking was; design the task so the thinking has to happen; make checking a visible skill; keep honesty a question you ask rather than a rule you police.
- Refer back to the first and second books naturally, two to five times per chapter, always by idea and story, never by page or chapter number.
- Length: 1,800 to 2,200 words for Part One chapters, 1,500 to 1,900 for Part Two field guides. Chapter 13 may run to 2,600.

```
🔍 Takeaway: One sentence.

## Section Title

Body...

**A vignette lead.** Story...

> A prompt the reader can copy.

🔍 Takeaway: (optional)

## Another Section

...

## A Quiet Pattern Worth Noticing

One or two paragraphs drawing the chapter's people together and naming the pattern.

## 🌱 Chapter N Reflective Pause

"Think of the last time..." Three to five short paragraphs. Ends with "You don't need to ... Just notice ..."

## 🌱 Chapter N: One Small Habit to Carry Forward

"For the next week, ..." One tiny action, seconds long. Three to five short paragraphs. Closes with a line about not needing a better tool, only a better question / a smaller habit.


---
```

Part Two field guides use this section order after the opening story and takeaway: `## What This Subject Quietly Asks` (or a chapter-specific equivalent named in your brief), two or three subject-specific sections, `## Three Prompts That Work Here` (three vignettes each with a blockquote prompt), `## The Trap`, `## A Quiet Pattern Worth Noticing`, then the Reflective Pause and One Small Habit.

Epigraphs: choose a real, well-known, accurately attributed quotation relevant to the chapter. If unsure of exact wording, use "Attributed to". Do not invent quotations. Do not reuse Picasso, Kettering, Matisse, Skinner (used in the second book).

## After the chapter

After the final `---`, add a section headed exactly `<!-- APPENDIX PROMPTS -->` containing four prompts for the appendix in this exact format (single sentence each, may use a semicolon, followed by a one-line "Use when..." line):

```
> Prompt sentence.

Use when ...
```

Then a line `<!-- END -->`. The appendix block will be lifted out by the editor.

## Continuity rules

- Do not reuse the exact stories from the exemplar chapters (fractions lesson plan, essay "Write this", the parent moving a daughter to a different school, the newsletter cover image). You may refer to them as things "the second book" described.
- Use the recurring cast where it fits, but each chapter should have its own new people: a Primary 5 teacher called Mrs Tan may appear across chapters only if your brief names her. Otherwise keep people unnamed ("a science teacher I know", "a father of two").
- Numbers and specifics: choose them and commit ("twenty-eight children", "nine seconds", "eleven questions, and she threw out four").
- Every chapter mentions at least once what stays on the human side: the choosing, the weighing, the deciding.
- Write the whole chapter in one file. No preamble, no notes to the editor, no headings outside the skeleton.
