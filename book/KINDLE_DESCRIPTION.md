# Kindle listing copy: Teaching the Right Question

## Book description

Every student now carries an answer key.

A worksheet can be finished in the time it takes to photograph it. An essay arrives before the title has been read. Twenty-seven of twenty-eight homework books come back perfect, and the teacher learns nothing about anyone in the room.

*Teaching the Right Question* is the third book in the series that began with *Asking the Right Question* and continued with *Asking the Right Prompt*. The first book was about asking better questions of people. The second carried those habits into the chat window. This one brings them into the classroom and to the kitchen table, where the tools were already sitting before anyone asked them in.

Its argument is simple. When answers are free, the answer stops being worth teaching. What remains is the question a student asks, the checking they do, and the judgement they bring to what comes back. Those three things can be taught the way anything is taught: in small, repeated moments, by teachers and parents who are willing to slow down.

Part One works in any subject and at any level. It shows how to set tasks that only thinking can finish, how to teach children to ask instead of only answer, how to make checking a visible skill on the page, how to mark with a tool without losing the student, and what to say at the table when a child announces that this isn't how the teacher did it.

Part Two is a set of short field guides: Science, Mathematics and English, each with prompts a teacher or student can copy tonight, followed by chapters on building small classroom tools with AI, on assessment and honesty in an age of instant answers, and on what stays on the teacher's side of the desk.

As in the earlier books, every chapter opens with a story rather than a theory, ends with a reflective pause and one small habit that takes seconds, and is written to be read slowly, with a class in mind. An appendix collects fifty prompts worth keeping.

For teachers, parents and older students who suspect that the most important thing in the room has never been the answer.
